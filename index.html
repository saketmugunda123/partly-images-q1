What each category gets

Each bar is 100% of the SKUs in that category. Blue, orange and green need a real part in front of a camera; yellow and pink do not.

Oil, Service and Wear parts are sold under many part numbers, so one photo is reused many times through $1 rebrands. Crash and Repair parts have one or two part numbers each, so a photo has to pay for itself.

The numbers by category

Share of SKUs getting each image, the shoots needed, and what it costs and earns.

Shoots = photos plus 3D builds. Return = (revenue − cost) ÷ cost.

The rule behind the mix

A SKU gets a better image only when it sells enough to pay for it. These are the yearly sales a SKU needs through customers' sites, before any image.

Where the money goes

A small share of SKUs earns most of the revenue.

What is held fixed, and how far to trust this
  • From the case: SKU, G-code and brand counts; market size by category; the seven image options with their costs and uplifts.
  • Held fixed here: Partly earns 2% of the extra sales an image creates; an image earns for 3 years; "part type" in Option 4 means the five categories; aftermarket SKUs cluster on popular parts; every SKU gets at least a text image; no limit on how many parts can be photographed.
  • Method: the page simulates a sample of physical parts in each category (about 0.8 million SKUs), gives each SKU long-tailed sales scaled to market size × the share through customers' sites, and picks the best-value image plan for every physical part. Results are scaled to all 71.1M SKUs.
  • Accuracy: this is a lighter version of the full model behind the written answer. Across 11 test settings of these three sliders, every image share is within 0.7 percentage points of the full model and cost within 3%.
  • Revenue depends directly on sliders 2 and 3. The ranking of image types and the rule are the robust part.
  • Not modelled: SKUs that never sell, SKUs that already have an image, and the cost of getting a part in front of a camera.
